Stop stacking GNN layers and hoping for the best — oversmoothing, oversquashing, and under-reaching are probably sabotaging your model long before you hit “train.”

Although Graph Neural Networks (GNNs) have demonstrated strong performance on various graph-related tasks, their message-passing scheme has limitations and drawbacks. These issues can be tackled with several techniques to enhance GNNs. Before delving deeper into this method, I’ll explain the three most common problems arising when building GNNs: oversmoothing, oversquashing, and under-reaching.

Oversmoothing, oversquashing, and under-reaching

At each GNN layer, the message-passing scheme aggregates the features of each node’s neighbors and inputs them into a non-linear activation function. This way, we’re able to update a node’s representation based on the graph signal and the graph structure.

However, when stacking many GNN layers (a common practice in many other deep learning architectures), node representations become increasingly similar, eventually “smoothing out” all distinctions across the graph.

Why does this happen? In GNNs, with each additional layer, information diffuses one more hop out. Indeed, at the first layer, a node receives information from its neighbors via the aggregation scheme. Then, at the second layer, since its neighbors received their neighbors’ information at the first layer, a node receives the information of its 2-hop neighborhood (i.e., its neighbors’ neighbors). After many layers, every node’s embedding converges toward the stationary distribution of this diffusion process, washing away the local structures and the graph signal.

In practice, oversmoothing constraints GNNs to have 5 layers or fewer.

Another problem related to GNN layer information aggregation is oversquashing. Oversquashing occurs when long-range signals from distant nodes in the graph are “squashed” into fixed-dimensional embeddings, rendering it impossible to propagate complex information across multiple hops.

To explain how it happens in detail, as you add layers, the number of source nodes grows exponentially, but you still compress their information into a vector of fixed size. This causes an information bottleneck where distinctions are lost. With oversquashing, the deeper the receptive field (the number of information source nodes), the worse the squashing.

To sum up, the number of information source nodes after K layers is the average degree to the power of K. All this information is stored in a vector of constant size, which leads to the loss of information because the vector does not have enough numbers to account for the distinction in information from all source nodes.

A quick solution that tackles both oversmoothing and oversquashing is to limit the number of layers in GNNs. Even though this solution works well, it also comes with the problem of under-reaching. Under-reaching stems from the fact that shallow GNNs (GNNs with few layers) can only aggregate information from nodes within their limited number of hops, hence failing to capture dependencies from farther nodes.

Knowing these three problem behaviors of GNNs is crucial in designing powerful graph models. Indeed, some of these behaviors can be harmless depending on the task at hand.

For instance, oversmoothing can be harmless in graph classification where GNNs are used to generate graph embeddings. In that case, any node representation (which will be the same for all nodes) can be used as a graph embedding. However, oversmoothing hurts tasks that rely on preserving differences between nodes, such as node classification in heterophilic graphs.

Tricks against oversmoothing

Oversmoothing seems to arise in GNNs because the message-passing scheme that we’ve described before depends too strongly on the incoming message aggregated from the neighbors at the expense of the node representations from the previous layer.

A natural way to tackle this issue is to induce a stronger bias on the node representation from the previous layer during the message-passing scheme. One way to do this is to use skip-connections, which try to preserve information from the previous node representation during the message-passing scheme.

There are several options to make skip connections. The simplest one is to concatenate the previous node representation with the output of the non-linear activation.

In addition to concatenation skip-connections, a solution would be to assign weights to the previous node representation and the output of the non-linear activation. This mechanism would allow the model to freely learn the weights and to give more importance to whether the previous node representation or the output of the non-linear activation, depending on the task.

A complementary approach to enhance the quality of the final node representations is to utilize the representations from each message-passing layer, rather than relying solely on the output of the final layer. This is known as jumping knowledge. In the jumping knowledge approach, the representations from each message-passing layer are concatenated and input into an arbitrary differentiable function.

So far, we have primarily focused on the most common form of GNN message passing, which primarily operates at the node level. However, the message passing framework for GNNs can be extended to incorporate edge-level and graph-level information at each step of the process. This approach is known as Generalised Message Passing.

First, let’s remember that to encode more information in our graph than just with node features, we can also add edge features and even a graph feature.

The Generalised Message Passing approach sequentially updates the edges, nodes, and graph representation. It starts by updating the edge representations based on their previous representations, the previous representations of the nodes that composed the edges, and the previous representation of the graph.

Then, the approach updates the nodes’ representation by a modified version of the message passing scheme. Instead of using the representation of a node’s neighbors to update its representation, it uses the representation of the adjacent edges in the message passing.

Finally, the updated representations of nodes and edges are used to update the graph representation in the final step of the generalised message passing.

GNNs limitations

Previously, we have focused on the theoretical limits of GNNs without also taking into account the practical limits of GNNs. Amongst these limits, we can identify 3 main problems: redundant computations, the memory footprint of GNNs, and the uselessness of pre-training.

Redundant computations appear when using node-level message passing equations. Implementing a GNN directly based on these equations can lead to computational inefficiencies. For instance, if multiple nodes share common neighbors, performing message passing operations independently for each node may result in repeated and unnecessary calculations.

To deal with this issue, the most efficient approach to reduce the number of mathematical operations required for message passing is to implement the GNN equations at the graph level.

However, in some cases, as in the Generalised Message Passing, the message passing mechanism is too complex to be reduced to a graph-level equation, leading to computational inefficiencies in the implementation of these methods.

Another important aspect to keep in mind when dealing with GNNs is their memory footprint as the number of nodes and edges grows larger. To reduce the memory usage of a GNN and enable mini-batch training, message passing can be performed on a subset of nodes. Mathematically, this involves applying the node-level GNN equations to a selected subset of nodes in the graph for each batch. By carefully designing the implementation, redundant computations can be avoided, ensuring that the embedding for each node in the batch is computed at most once during model execution.

The challenge with this approach lies in the fact that running message passing on only a subset of nodes in a graph can result in information loss because removing a node also eliminates its edges, effectively altering the adjacency matrix. Additionally, randomly selecting a subset of nodes does not ensure the resulting graph will remain connected, and therefore, using a random subset of nodes for each mini-batch can significantly degrade model performance.

Hamilton et al. [4] proposed a strategy to address this issue by sub-sampling node neighborhoods. The method involves first selecting a set of target nodes for a batch and then recursively sampling their neighbors to preserve the graph’s connectivity. To prevent excessive node sampling within a batch, they suggest sub-sampling each node’s neighbors using a fixed sample size, which enhances the efficiency of batched tensor operations. For more detailed explanations of how GraphSAGE works, I recommend reading another article in the series, Mastering Graph ML: GNNs and the message-passing framework.

The last main limitation of GNNs in practice is pre-training. In the last few years, pre-training has become a standard component of deep learning pipelines, especially with LLMs. Hence, it is natural to consider whether pre-training with neighborhood reconstruction objectives could improve performance on downstream classification tasks for GNNs.

Most unsupervised GNN pre-training methods aim either to maximize mutual information across different representation levels or to distinguish real node–embedding pairs from corrupted ones. Surprisingly, these approaches have shown limited effectiveness for GNNs. Veličković et al. [2], for instance, reported that randomly initialized GNNs perform on par with models pre-trained using neighborhood reconstruction losses.

A common explanation is that message passing already embeds substantial neighborhood information: nodes that are close in the graph naturally receive similar representations, making explicit neighborhood reconstruction somewhat redundant. Despite this, developing more effective GNN pre-training methods remains an active and open research direction.

Conclusions

In this article, we unpacked the core pathologies of GNNs — oversmoothing, oversquashing, and under-reaching — and saw how they fundamentally constrain depth and expressivity along with the often-overlooked computational and practical limits of GNNs. We then walked through practical design tricks to make GNNs more robust and informative. Taken together, these ideas provide a roadmap for using today’s GNNs more effectively.
