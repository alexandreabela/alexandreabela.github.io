From social networks to molecular structures, how do we “fake” the complexity of real-world graphs? Discover the foundational math and logic behind the traditional models that first taught us how to generate graphs.

So far in this series, we’ve focused on graph representation learning, but there is also a complementary task that deserves our attention: graph generation. The goal here is to create realistic graph structures. This can be viewed as the “mirror” problem of graph representation learning, whereas representation learning maps a graph to a vector space, graph generation maps a distribution or a latent code back into a graph

Generating graphs isn’t inherently difficult. After all, it is trivial to build a fully-connected graph or a random one. However, these structures are rarely useful because real-world graphs possess specific, “desirable” properties. The true challenge lies in duplicating these intricate properties to create synthetic graphs that are indistinguishable from the real ones.

Traditional approaches to graph generation generally involve specifying a generative process, which defines how the edges in a graph are created. In general, this process boils down to sampling each potential edge based on a specified probability or likelihood

The downside of this simple model is that it rarely generates realistic graphs. Since all edges are sampled uniformly and independently, the model fails to produce the community structures often observed in social networks or biological systems.

Graph Generation and Evaluation

To evaluate the quality of the generated graphs, the current practice is to analyze different statistics of the generated graphs and to compare the distribution of statistics for the generated graphs to a test set.
For instance, we can compute the distance between the statistic’s distribution on the test graph and the generated graph using a distributional measure, such as the total variation (TV) distance.

More clearly, the TV distance measures the overlap between two probability distributions. For example, if one looks at the node degree, calculating the TV distance can be thought of as looking at every possible value (e.g., nodes with degree 5), finding the difference in how often that value appears in the real vs. synthetic datasets, and taking the maximum of these differences. Hence, a TV distance of 0 means the distributions are identical, and a TV distance of 1 means they have nothing in common.

Applications and Limits

The first useful application of these generative models is that they can be used for synthetic graphs for benchmarking and analysis tasks. In a statistical sense, they provide us with a null models that we can use as reference points for our understanding of real-world graphs.

However, these approaches come with three main disadvantages:

  • These approaches can generate graphs, but they cannot learn a generative model from data; therefore, we need prior knowledge of the graphs we want to generate, which may not be available for some tasks.
  • Also, real graphs aren’t just dots and lines; nodes have features, and traditional models struggle to generate these attributes alongside the structure.
  • Finally, real-world graphs often exhibit “hierarchical” structures (communities within communities), and traditional approaches are usually too flat to capture them.

In other words, traditional models like Erdös-Renyi, Stochastic Block Models, or Preferential Attachment are quite useful in their simplicity, but they require us to guess beforehand the underlying rules of a network before we can simulate it. In real world scenarii, graphs are messy, often multi-layered, and, in some cases, massive, which makes guessing the underlying rules really hard. This is where Deep Generative Methods come in handy, as we’ll later see in another article.
